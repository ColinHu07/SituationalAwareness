# Biggest social struggles autistic teens and adults report, ranked by evidence

Scope note: about 20 search/fetch calls. Several key papers (Springer, SAGE) were paywalled, so some numbers come from abstracts or search snippets, not full texts. These are flagged. The ranking below is my own synthesis. No single study ranks all of these struggles against each other.

Evidence labels used below: **[Exp]** = experiment. **[Survey]** = self-report survey. **[Qual]** = interviews or themes, usually small n. **[Review]** = systematic review. **[Weak]** = blog, vendor, or aggregator source.

## Q1. What do autistic adults and teens themselves name as their top social challenges?

### Takeaway
When autistic adults are asked openly, "socializing" is the most-named difficulty, but only about 1 in 5 name it first. Their stories focus less on missing skills and more on: not knowing the unwritten rules, being misunderstood, noisy group settings, phone calls, first impressions, and the cost of hiding their autism. Evidence is mostly self-report and qualitative. Experiments back up a few specific points: speech in noise, first impressions, and mixed-neurotype rapport.

### Working ranking (synthesis; strength of evidence in brackets)

**Real-time, in-the-moment struggles**
1. **Following speech in noisy or multi-talker settings (group chats, restaurants, meetings, classrooms).** Strong. A self-report survey found 85–88% difficulty [Survey, n=79], and a systematic review of experiments backs it up [Review/Exp].
2. **Unwritten rules of new settings and workplace decorum (what topics are OK, boundaries, first contact).** Moderate. This theme shows up across many qualitative studies [Qual].
3. **Being misread or judged within seconds (tone, facial expression, "awkward").** Strong. Experiments show this happens [Exp, Sasson 2017], and autistic people describe feeling "very misunderstood" [Qual].
4. **Phone calls, where there is no processing time or visual cues and calls are unexpected.** Moderate. One well-known survey titled "Anything but the phone!" [Survey; full text blocked].
5. **Implied meaning: sarcasm/irony more than indirect requests.** Moderate, and mixed. Experiments suggest irony is harder than indirect requests. Common indirect requests ("Can you pass…") seem fine. Hints like "It's cold in here" need more inference [Exp].
6. **Starting conversations and small talk, and selling oneself in interviews.** Moderate [Qual].
7. **Delayed processing (thinking of the reply too late) and replaying the conversation afterward.** Weak. Mostly community/blog sources. I found no peer-reviewed prevalence figures.
8. **Turn-taking, knowing how to leave a conversation, remembering names, volume/prosody feedback.** Weak or not found as separate self-report themes. They may sit inside broader "communication struggles" themes, but I found no dedicated prevalence data.

**Structural struggles**
A. **Masking/camouflaging and the exhaustion and burnout it causes.** Strong, consistent qualitative and review evidence.
B. **Loneliness despite wanting social contact.** Strong [Review, 34 studies].
C. **Employment barriers: interviews, stigma, disclosure risk.** Strong [Review + Qual + stats].
D. **Stigma and negative first impressions from non-autistic people.** Strong [Exp].

### Cited Findings
- In an open-ended survey of autistic young adults, the most-cited primary difficulty was "Socializing" (21.8%). Next came Executive Functioning (14%), Co-occurring Mental Health (12.6%), and Financial Challenges (12.6%). About 294 people answered the difficulty question. [Survey; Springer paywalled, figures from abstract/snippet; data source (e.g., SPARK) not confirmed] — [Self-Reported Strengths and Difficulties by Autistic Young Adults, JADD 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10803-026-07260-0)
- Interviews with 15 autistic adults aged 18–28 (US) found 5 themes: communication struggles, comfort in familiar situations, a communication style others misunderstand, "the need to learn people," and recognizing shared humanity. [Qual, n=15] — [“Very Misunderstood”, Youth 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/youth4040104)
- Interviews with 17 autistic adults (mean age 26.7) found: first social contact is hard and leads to isolation; new environments are overstimulating; boundaries are hard because workplace decorum and suitable topics are unclear; co-workers bully; and people grow in their social and professional identity. [Qual, n=17; paywalled, from snippet] — [Social Skills Through an Autistic Lens, JADD 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10803-025-06869-x)
- One participant quote: "there's unwritten special rules that everyone has to follow." [Qual] — [“Unwritten Rules are Everywhere”, Int J Disability Dev Educ 2026](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1034912X.2026.2667971)
- Social anxiety study: autistic adults tied anxiety to the difficulty of starting and keeping up back-and-forth interaction. Sensory sensitivities came up in response to the social environment. [Qual] — [Social anxiety in adults with autism, IJQSHW 2020](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17482631.2020.1803669)
- Phone calls: a study of communication-mode preferences found 4 themes: "Not the Phone," Written Communication, Masking versus Autistic Communication, and Avoiding Communication. [Survey + open text; full text 403, so no % available] — [Howard & Sedgewick 2021, ‘Anything but the phone!’, Autism](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/13623613211014995)
- Implied language: autistic adults use context to decide whether an ambiguous remark is a request, much like non-autistic adults. [Exp] — [Autistic and non-autistic adults use discourse context to determine a speaker's intention to request (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11897084/)
- Another study found autistic adults understood indirect requests in context but had "marked difficulties" with irony. [Exp] — [Selective Pragmatic Impairment in ASD: Indirect Requests Versus Irony, JADD 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10803-018-3561-6)
- Delayed processing and rumination, described as "the reply you think of later." This is a blog, not research. [Weak] — [HeyASD blog](https://www.heyasd.com/blogs/autism/autistic-delayed-processing)
- Community research priorities (UK, 2016, over 1,000 people consulted): the top priorities were mental health interventions, anxiety, communication/language interventions, social care for adults, and environments/supports for "education/life/social skills outcomes." Social skills per se were not a standalone top priority. [Priority-setting] — [Autistica: Your research priorities](https://www.autistica.org.uk/our-research/your-research-priorities)

### Inferences
- The most useful "real-time" help targets are probably: (a) understanding speech in noise and groups, (b) decoding unwritten rules or implied meaning in the moment, and (c) processing time. Autistic people describe these as practical barriers, not as parts of themselves they want changed.
- Research priorities stress anxiety and mental health over "social skills." So a tool framed as reducing load or anxiety may fit better than one framed as fixing deficits.

### Gaps
- I found no large survey that directly ranks specific in-the-moment struggles against each other: turn-taking, exiting conversations, names, small talk, volume/prosody.
- Could not get the % who avoid phone calls (Howard & Sedgewick full text blocked).
- Could not confirm whether the JADD 2026 sample came from SPARK, or its age range.
- NAS and ASAN survey numbers on social difficulties were not retrieved.

## Q2. How common are auditory processing and speech-in-noise difficulties? Do captions help?

### Takeaway
Among autistic adults who took part in a listening study, most (85–96%) said noisy or multi-talker settings are hard. Experiments confirm the problem is specific. Steady noise is fine. Other voices, fluctuating noise, and audio-visual integration cause trouble. Captions are widely used and self-reported as helpful. However, I found no controlled trial of live captions for autistic people in conversation.

### Cited Findings
- Sample: 79 autistic adults (55 diagnosed, 24 seeking diagnosis), aged 18–55, 63% female. Self-selected, so prevalence may be overestimated. [Survey] — [The diversity of speech-perception difficulties among autistic individuals (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10822079/)
  - 88% rated listening with 1–2 background voices as "fairly challenging" or "very difficult." 85% rated many-talker settings "very difficult." 92% had trouble with loud mechanical noise, and 96% with loud music.
  - The difficulty had different causes for different people: focusing on voices amid background sound (22 people), separating the target voice (20), speech drowned out (23), loudness discomfort/overload (22), misophonia (6).
  - Quotes: "I don't seem to have the ability to focus on one sound, or block out unwanted sounds." / "I watch people's mouths to help understand." / "I use subtitles when watching tv/movies so I can process what is being said."
- Systematic review of experiments: speech perception was generally intact in steady noise. Problems appeared with fluctuating noise, competing speech, and audio-visual speech perception. These were linked to auditory timing processing and language skills. [Review] — [A systematic review on speech-in-noise perception in autism, Neurosci Biobehav Rev 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0149763423003755)
- EEG study: autistic participants showed weaker brain tracking of speech and a delayed N400 (a brain signal of meaning processing). This fits with slower meaning-level processing of speech in noise. [Exp] — [Auditory and Semantic Processing of Speech-in-Noise in Autism (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12541682/)
- Autistic adults' brains used recent sound patterns less to shape speech processing. This was linked to how well they understood speech in background noise. [Exp] — [Reduced Use of Prior Sound Patterns (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13370381/)
- Autistic traits and speech perception in social vs non-social noise. [Exp] — [Scientific Reports 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-52050-2)
- Online accounts by autistic adults (Davies, cited in the review) showed 3 themes: auditory overwhelm, difficulty picking out speech from background noise, and rich processing of soundscapes. [Qual] — [via PMC10822079](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10822079/)
- Audio-visual speech: autistic and non-autistic adults got similar behavioral benefit from seeing the speaker, even though their brain responses differed. [Exp] — [Multisensory Integration of Naturalistic Speech and Gestures in Autistic Adults (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12166514/)
- Captions: a vendor blog says many autistic people mute content and use captions. [Weak; vendor marketing] — [3Play Media](https://www.3playmedia.com/blog/how-captioned-videos-help-autistic-students/)

### Inferences
- The difficulty is concentrated exactly where live conversation happens: competing talkers, groups, cafés. This is the strongest-supported target for a real-time aid.
- Captions for live speech are a reasonable idea given the self-reports ("subtitles so I can process"). But the benefit in live conversation is not yet shown by experiment.

### Gaps
- I found no population-level prevalence of auditory processing disorder or speech-in-noise problems in autism. Only a self-selected survey (n=79).
- I found no controlled study of live captions or smart-glasses captions for autistic users.
- I found no hard data on "delayed processing" (response latency in conversation) as self-reported prevalence.

## Q3. What do autistic people want help with, and what do they not want?

### Takeaway
They want practical, adult-context, on-demand support: workplace communication, anxiety in real settings, dating, bars, shops. They also want acceptance and contact with other autistic people. They reject training that enforces neurotypical behaviors (eye contact, rigid rules) or feels like being made to mask. Across the evidence, "Just ask what support we need" sums it up.

### Cited Findings
- Interviews with 11 UK autistic adults (ages 20–58, mostly diagnosed as adults). [Qual, n=11] — [Hull et al. 2025, “Just Ask What Support We Need”, Autism in Adulthood (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12174831/)
  - **Wanted:** practical life skills (shopping, pubs), managing social anxiety in real settings, workplace communication, adult contexts (dating, bars, work), understanding and accepting their autism, and connecting with autistic peers. They wanted support that is personalized and available "as and when needed."
  - **Did not want:** eye-contact and personal-space drills ("I kind of understood what personal space was, I don't really need that"), or training that pushes masking ("It can feel a bit like it's conversion therapy, like it's trying to change who you are"). They also objected to the stigma implied by autistic-only training ("If there's training just for autistic people, it says autistic people aren't good at socializing").
- In a social-validity study, 99% of autistic adults agreed that interventionists should support communication by any technique that works, not only speech. [Survey; from snippet] — [The Social Validity of Behavioral Interventions: Seeking Input from Autistic Adults (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11933200/)
- Autistic adults often prefer online or written communication. It has benefits, but also risks: anxiety and being bullied online. [RCT pilot background] — [SELFI pilot RCT (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11461777/)
- Double-empathy evidence:
  - The original 2020 diffusion-chain study (n=72) found info passed well in all-autistic chains. Mixed chains lost detail faster and had lower rapport. [Exp] — [Crompton et al. 2020, Autism](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1362361320919286)
  - A larger 2025 replication (n=311: 154 autistic, 157 non-autistic) found **no** difference in information transfer between chain types. Rapport was highest in non-autistic chains as rated by "teachers." Both groups of learners preferred same-neurotype partners. Telling partners about the diagnosis improved rapport. **The 2020 info-loss finding did not replicate.** — [Information transfer within and between autistic and non-autistic people, Nature Human Behaviour 2025 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12283400/)

### Inferences
- A tool that gives private, on-demand help (captions, context hints) in real settings fits what people say they want. A tool that coaches "look them in the eye" or "act normal" fits what they reject.
- Because disclosure improved rapport in the 2025 study, tools that support the user's own choices (like disclosing) may help more than tools that hide autistic traits.

### Gaps
- The samples are small (n=11). I found no large survey ranking desired kinds of help.
- I found nothing on what autistic people think of wearable or AI real-time social aids specifically. Privacy of bystanders and feeling "managed" are open questions.

## Q4. What does masking cost, and what reduces it?

### Takeaway
Autistic people mask to get jobs, get through school, make friends and partners, and avoid bullying. Across the studies I found, the cost is exhaustion, anxiety, depression, and losing a sense of self. Masking is linked to autistic burnout. Masking only some of the time may be as stressful as masking all the time. Things that seem to lower the cost: being accepted, disclosure that goes well, autistic peers, and less demanding environments. Evidence on what reduces masking is thinner than evidence on its costs.

### Cited Findings
- Hull et al. 2017: 92 autistic adults. They masked to fit in and connect with others. Costs included exhaustion, challenging stereotypes, and threats to self-perception. Camouflaging has been proposed as one reason girls and women are diagnosed late or missed. [Qual, n=92] — [“Putting on My Best Normal”, JADD 2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10803-017-3166-5)
- Reasons for camouflaging: employment and education, friendships and romantic relationships, and avoiding bullying and ostracism. [Review] — [Camouflaging in autism: A systematic review, Clin Psych Rev 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0272735821001239)
- Cage & Troxell-Whitman's finding: people who switch between masking and not masking spend energy judging the risk of being "found out" in each setting. This may be as stressful as masking all the time. [Survey; described via secondary source] — [Durben 2024, Understanding autistic camouflaging](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/27546330241266726)
- Systematic review of the consequences of camouflaging in autistic adults: mental health harms including exhaustion, anxiety, depression, and loss of identity. [Review] — [The consequences of social camouflaging in autistic adults, 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S3050656525000288)
- A 2026 systematic review looks at camouflaging and autistic burnout together. [Review; abstract only] — [Autistic people's experience of camouflaging and autistic burnout in relation to one another](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S3050579826000112)
- Autistic burnout (Raymaker et al. 2020, AASPIRE, interviews plus online sources): long-lasting exhaustion (typically 3+ months), loss of function, and lower tolerance to stimuli. It comes from chronic life stress and a mismatch between what is expected and what the person can do without enough support. The authors warn this points to dangers in teaching autistic people to mask. [Qual] — [Raymaker et al. 2020 (PDXScholar)](https://pdxscholar.library.pdx.edu/socwork_fac/378/); [NAS summary](https://www.autism.org.uk/learn/knowledge-hub/professional-practice/autistic-burnout)
- Masking in job interviews took great effort and led to stress, anxiety and exhaustion. A quote used as the paper's title: "If I'm just me, I doubt I'd get the job." [Qual] — [Finn et al. 2023, Autism](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13623613231153480)
- Relationships between autistic identity, disclosure and camouflaging. [Brief report] — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343162305_Brief_report_Understanding_the_relationships_between_autistic_identity_disclosure_and_camouflaging)

### Inferences
- If an aid lowers the mental work of monitoring the conversation (tracking speech, decoding hints), it may lower one cause of masking fatigue. If it raises self-monitoring (constant feedback on volume or eye contact), it may add to the load. This is my inference, not tested.

### Gaps
- I did not get the exact sample size or effect sizes from Cage & Troxell-Whitman 2019 directly.
- I found no intervention trials showing what reduces masking. The evidence on reduction is correlational or qualitative.

## Q5. Structural issues: loneliness, stigma, employment

### Takeaway
Autistic adults report high interest in social contact but high loneliness, and loneliness rises from the teen years into young adulthood. Non-autistic people form negative first impressions within seconds, driven by *style*, not *content*. Employment rates are low (around 14–29% by country) and social demands, not technical skill, are the main barrier.

### Cited Findings
- Systematic review of 34 studies: autistic adults report high social interest and high rates of loneliness. Measures built for the general public may not fit. [Review] — [Grace et al. 2022, Loneliness in autistic adults, Autism](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/13623613221077721)
- Loneliness increased from adolescence to young adulthood, possibly because there are fewer structured social opportunities after school. [Longitudinal] — [Longitudinal study of loneliness in autism (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11132953/)
- Sasson et al. 2017: non-autistic observers rated autistic people less favorably within seconds, especially on likeability and awkwardness, but not on trustworthiness or intelligence. Observers were less willing to interact with them. The bias disappeared when observers saw only what was said, without audio or video. The authors concluded that style, not substance, drives the negative first impressions. [Exp] — [Scientific Reports 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep40700)
- First impressions improved when the diagnosis was disclosed and when observers knew more about autism. [Exp] — [Sasson & Morrison 2019, Autism](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1362361317729526)
- Employment rates by country: Australia 27.3%, Israel 28%, UK 29%, US/Canada 14%. [Aggregator — Wikipedia; treat as approximate] — [Employment of autistic people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Employment_of_autistic_people)
- Outside autism-specific jobs, social and communication problems were the most-mentioned job barriers. [Survey] — [Autism and Overcoming Job Barriers (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4713226/)
- Interviews are hard because of implicit rules and the need to "sell yourself." [Qual] — [Finn et al. 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13623613231153480)
- Workplace disclosure: people disclosed mainly to be understood and accepted, but doing so risks discrimination. [Qual] — [Romualdez et al. 2021, Autism in Adulthood](https://doi.org/10.1089/AUT.2020.0063)
- Integrative review of barriers: low autism awareness, communication challenges, stigma, inflexible practices, and poor manager support. [Review] — [JADD 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10803-026-07467-1)

### Inferences
- Much of the "social difficulty" sits in how non-autistic people react (the Sasson findings, double empathy). An aid only for the autistic user cannot fix structural stigma, and may be seen as asking autistic people to do all the adapting.

### Gaps
- The "71% of UK autistic adults unemployed" figure comes only from aggregator snippets. It was not verified against the original ONS/NAS source, so I left it out.

## Q6. Subgroup differences (teens vs adults, late-diagnosed, women, ADHD/alexithymia)

### Takeaway
There is limited direct evidence from this search. Loneliness rises from teens into young adulthood. Camouflaging is tied to women and late diagnosis. Samples in speech-in-noise and social-skills studies are skewed toward women and adults diagnosed later in life. I found no usable data on ADHD or alexithymia.

### Cited Findings
- Loneliness rises from adolescence into young adulthood. [Longitudinal] — [PMC11132953](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11132953/)
- Camouflaging has been proposed as one reason females are diagnosed late or missed. [Qual] — [Hull et al. 2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10803-017-3166-5)
- Women diagnosed in middle or late adulthood described being "exhausted trying to figure it out." [Qual] — [Leedham et al. 2020, Autism](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362361319853442)
- The speech-in-noise survey was 63% female, and 24 of 79 people were still seeking diagnosis. So its high rates may reflect a late-diagnosed, female-heavy sample. [Survey] — [PMC10822079](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10822079/)
- The "Just ask" social-skills study sample was mostly diagnosed in adulthood. [Qual] — [PMC12174831](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12174831/)
- A study of neurodivergent women in software engineering describes an "invisible load." [Qual; preprint] — [arXiv 2512.05350](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.05350)

### Inferences
- The people most likely to be early users of a discreet aid (late-diagnosed adults, often women, who mask heavily) are also the most represented in this research base.

### Gaps
- I found no data comparing autistic people with and without co-occurring ADHD or alexithymia on these specific social struggles. The search did not cover this; it needs a targeted search.
- I found no teen-specific self-report ranking of social struggles. Teen evidence here is only the loneliness trajectory.
- I found no data on sex differences in speech-in-noise difficulty.
