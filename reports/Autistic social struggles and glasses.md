# Aside should translate, not correct, autistic conversation

Aside's best fit is not "reading faces" or "teaching social skills". It fits three things autistic adults say are hard and that it can actually help with: **following speech in noisy or group settings**, **working out implied meaning and the unwritten rules of a setting**, and **getting a moment to think before and after a conversation**. The strongest self-report evidence is on noise: in one survey of **79 autistic adults, 85–88% found group or background-voice listening hard**. Experiments back up the problem, but no study yet shows that live captions fix it in real conversation. Past glasses aids for autism (Stanford's Superpower Glass, Brain Power) rested on emotion-reading from faces. They showed small, short-lived gains, lost about half their users, and drew sharp criticism from autistic scholars. Aside already avoids face ID and emotion guessing, which puts it on the right side of that history. Its hard limits are clear: a **~10 s model delay** (roughly 12–17 s from words to cue, estimated, not measured) rules out turn-taking help. The ambient-audio path that hears conversation partners is **dev/beta only and cannot ship to production**. The model's API terms **require adult users**, so teens are out of scope for now. The first things to build are wearer-requested "what did that mean?" help, pre-event briefings, wearer-written scripts and notes, and sensory-friendly setting cues. Aside should avoid pushing a steady stream of behavior tips. The main open question is whether glanceable cues during a conversation help autistic users or just add load. No study has tested that yet.

## Noise, snap judgments, and hidden rules top the struggle list

No single study ranks autistic social struggles against each other. The ranking below is a synthesis across studies of different quality. When autistic young adults were asked an open question, "socializing" was the most-named main difficulty, but **only 21.8% named it first** (about 294 people answered; paywalled, figures from the abstract) ([JADD 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10803-026-07260-0)). When they describe it in detail, the problem is rarely "missing skills". It is noisy rooms, unclear rules, being misread, and the cost of hiding their autism.

| Rank | Struggle | Evidence grade | Key evidence | Aside fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Following speech in noise and groups | **Strong** (survey + experiments) | 88% found 1–2 background voices hard; 85% rated many-talker settings "very difficult" (n=79, self-selected, 63% female) ([PMC10822079](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10822079/)). A review of experiments found trouble with competing speech and changing noise, not steady noise ([Neurosci Biobehav Rev 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0149763423003755)) | Good in principle (captions), untested |
| 2 | Being misjudged within seconds | **Strong** (experiments) | Non-autistic observers rated autistic people as more awkward and less likeable within seconds. The bias vanished when observers saw only the words ([Sasson 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep40700)) | Poor: this is mostly other people's bias |
| 3 | Masking and the burnout it causes | **Strong** (qualitative + reviews) | Masking is linked to exhaustion, anxiety, depression, and loss of self ([review 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S3050656525000288)); burnout typically lasts 3+ months ([Raymaker 2020](https://pdxscholar.library.pdx.edu/socwork_fac/378/)) | Risky: a coach could add to it |
| 4 | Loneliness despite wanting contact | **Strong** (review of 34 studies) | High social interest and high loneliness ([Grace 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/13623613221077721)); loneliness rises from teens into young adulthood ([PMC11132953](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11132953/)) | Indirect only |
| 5 | Work: interviews, stigma, disclosure | **Strong** (review + stats) | Employment roughly 14–29% by country (Wikipedia, approximate) ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Employment_of_autistic_people)); social demands are the most-named job barrier ([PMC4713226](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4713226/)) | Partial (briefings, talking points) |
| 6 | Unwritten rules of new settings | **Moderate** (qualitative, small n) | Unclear workplace decorum, topics, and boundaries (n=17) ([JADD 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10803-025-06869-x)); "there's unwritten special rules that everyone has to follow" ([IJDDE 2026](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1034912X.2026.2667971)) | **Strong** |
| 7 | Implied meaning, especially irony | **Moderate, mixed** (experiments) | Indirect requests understood in context; "marked difficulties" with irony ([JADD 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10803-018-3561-6); [PMC11897084](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11897084/)) | Good for idioms and vague references; weak for sarcasm |
| 8 | Phone calls | **Moderate** (survey, % not retrieved) | Themes include "Not the Phone" and a preference for written communication ([Howard & Sedgewick 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/13623613211014995)) | Partial (Meta already captions calls) |
| 9 | Starting conversations, small talk | **Moderate** (qualitative) | First contact is hard and leads to isolation ([JADD 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10803-025-06869-x)) | Partial (wearer-invoked openers) |
| 10 | Slow processing, replaying talks afterward | **Weak** (blogs only) | No peer-reviewed prevalence found ([HeyASD](https://www.heyasd.com/blogs/autism/autistic-delayed-processing)) | Good fit (debrief), but the need is unproven |
| — | Turn-taking, exiting, names, volume | **Not found** as separate themes | No dedicated data | Mostly poor (see below) |

Two things change how to read this table. First, the "social problem" is two-sided. A 2020 study (n=72) found that information passed well between autistic people and got lost faster in mixed autistic/non-autistic groups ([Crompton 2020](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1362361320919286)). A **2025 replication about four times larger (n=311) did not find the information loss**. Only the gap in comfort and rapport held up, and telling partners about the diagnosis improved rapport ([Nature Human Behaviour 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12283400/)). So the evidence suggests mixed conversations mostly break down in comfort and first impressions, not in the facts being passed on. A tool used only by the autistic person cannot fix the other side's bias. Second, autistic research priorities put **anxiety and mental health ahead of "social skills"** (UK, 1,000+ people consulted) ([Autistica](https://www.autistica.org.uk/our-research/your-research-priorities)). In interviews (n=11), people asked for practical, on-demand help in real adult settings such as work, pubs, and dating. They rejected eye-contact drills and training that felt "a bit like it's conversion therapy" ([Hull 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12174831/)). Teen evidence is thin. The notes found no teen-specific self-report ranking, only the rise in loneliness. Samples lean toward women and people diagnosed as adults, who are also the likely early users of a discreet aid.

## Earlier glasses failed on emotion-reading and dropout, not on comfort

**Superpower Glass** is the only randomized trial of a glasses-based social aid. Seventy-one children aged 6–12 were split into 40 who used Google Glass with emotion cues plus usual therapy and 31 who got usual therapy only. After 6 weeks, parents rated social skills **4.58 points higher** on the Vineland socialization scale (P = .005). But **by 12 weeks the gain was gone** (P = .26). Objective emotion-recognition tests did not move (NEPSY-II P = .86), and parents knew which group their child was in ([JAMA Pediatrics](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2728462)). **35 of 71 dropped out**, children used it about half the recommended time, and one expert called it "basically science's use as a marketing exercise" ([The Transmitter](https://www.thetransmitter.org/spectrum/tech-firms-superpower-glass-autism-not-super-experts-say/)). An earlier home study found children's interest steadily fell ([PMC6550272](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6550272/)). **Brain Power** reported gains in small, mostly company-run studies. Its most solid finding is simply that the glasses were tolerable: **16 of 18 people (89%) could wear and use them, and 14 of 16 reported no negative effects** ([PMC6111791](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6111791/)).

The science behind **emotion AI** is weak. A major 2019 review found facial movements do not reliably map to emotions across people and contexts ([Barrett et al.](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1529100619832930)). Microsoft pulled emotion inference from Azure Face in 2022–2023 for that reason ([Azure](https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/blog/responsible-ai-investments-and-safeguards-for-facial-recognition/)). In one study of autistic adult women, trouble recognizing emotions tracked alexithymia (difficulty identifying feelings), not autism ([PMC7539601](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7539601/)). In Microsoft's video-call prototype, autistic adults doubted its accuracy and wanted more complex states than 6 basic emotions. They did not know how to act on the readings, and found cues "a little distracting". Yet some said "1000% I want this" and asked for help reading the *partner* ([Begel 2020](https://andrewbegel.com/papers/lessons-learned-autism.pdf)). Cambridge's smart-glasses project is still at the design stage, with results expected October 2028 ([ARC](https://www.autismresearchcentre.com/projects/smart-glasses/)).

**Captions** are the most mature and least disputed glasses feature. But they were built for Deaf and hard-of-hearing users. For autistic people or people with auditory processing disorder, only testimonials and marketing exist, no studies ([XRAI review](https://ixd.prattsi.org/2023/09/assistive-technology-xrai-glass-app-glasses/)). Meta Ray-Ban Display already ships English live captions for the person you face, plus call captions ([Meta help](https://www.meta.com/help/ai-glasses/23879220601763496/)). **LLM assistants** show real demand: **9 of 11 autistic workers preferred a GPT-4 advisor to a hidden human**, but a job coach judged some of its advice unsuitable, such as "just walk up to people and start talking" ([Jang et al. CHI 2024](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3613904.3642894); [CMU News](https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2024/june/people-with-autism-turn-to-chatgpt-for-advice-on-workplace-issues)). ChatGPT's advice reflects neurotypical social norms ([CHI 2025](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3706598.3713420)). All of this is from slow, typed use such as drafting and rehearsing. No peer-reviewed study was found of a live LLM copilot used by autistic people. Across training apps and VR, lab gains are medium to large (g = 0.81–0.93), but carrying them into real life is the common weak point ([JTBS 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41347-020-00177-0); [PMC12387758](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12387758/)). An in-the-moment aid sidesteps that problem, but its benefit may also stop when the glasses come off.

The lessons for Aside: expect users to stop using it after the novelty fades, and measure use at 1–3 months. Don't claim benefit from unblinded ratings. Design for adults who choose the tool, not for therapy imposed on children.

## Autistic critics want a two-way translator the wearer controls

Autistic and critical-HCI scholars have measured the field's bias. **90% of the wearable autism tools reviewed were "normalizing" technologies**, meaning tools that try to make autistic people act more neurotypical, and 50% focused on social-skills training or emotion assessment ([Williams & Gilbert 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1071581920300872)). Autistic children often end up a "secondary audience" to goals set by parents and therapists ([Spiel et al. 2019](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3344919)). Autism has been used as a showcase case to build emotion AI and surveillance products ([Nagy 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/14614448221109550)). The masking concern applies directly to a glasses coach. Masking includes "preparing scripts for social interactions" and forced eye contact, and it is linked to burnout and suicidality ([Pearson & Rose 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1089/aut.2020.0043)). An analysis of **3,984 autistic social-media posts** about ChatGPT found real benefits, such as translating neurotypical messages and calming down. It also found a risk of "identity erasure through masking". The authors recommended "bidirectional translation" and "beneficial friction" ([arXiv 2601.17946](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.17946)). It is an inference, not a tested result, that live "smile now / say X" prompts would add to the masking load. The user would have to watch the conversation, the display, and their own performance at once.

What autistic adults ask for is consistent. In interviews with **106 autistic adults in Chile**, technology supported self-determination when it kept the user in charge of decisions, offered simple refusal and reversible settings, protected attention, and respected privacy and sensory needs. Agency was undermined by settings imposed by others, opaque automation, and too many notifications ([PubMed 41231666](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41231666/)). Recent work reframes the goal from "Can they do it?" to "Do they get to decide?" ([Jang, Carrington & Begel 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07737)). Mixed-pair co-design also points at the *pair*, not only the autistic person ([Zolyomi 2023](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3610057)).

These sources add up to a set of design principles. Help should be **pull-based and private**: the user asks, and the answer appears quietly. Interpretations should be framed as possibilities, not facts ("might be a joke; you could ask"). Scripts should be written and triggered by the user, never pushed as the "correct" behavior. Data should stay with the wearer only, with no parent or employer dashboards. Sensory-first defaults matter: dim, sparse, and a quiet mode. Bystanders need protection: no face ID, no stored faces or audio. Any disclosure should be the user's choice. Success should never be measured as "more socializing" or "better masking". Aside's current stance of no face ID, no emotion or intent guessing, and dismiss/pause/stop controls already meets several of these ([server/model.mjs](../server/model.mjs)). One gap is the default. Aside pushes automatic cues (at most one every 30 s), which leans toward the "notification stream" the Chile participants disliked ([docs/ARCHITECTURE.md](../docs/ARCHITECTURE.md)). No community reaction data exists yet on AI glasses coaches specifically. Reddit and Bluesky threads were not found.

## Aside's 10-second loop fits slow help, not turn-by-turn help

Aside's facts decide what is possible. It sends one low-res image (refreshed every 2 s) plus recently transcribed speech to a model. A check runs at most every 8 s, and cues are capped at 14 words, shown for 8 s, with a 30 s cooldown. Synthetic cue requests took **9.51–10.09 s**, and the estimated time from words to cue is **~12–17 s** ([README.md](../README.md); [docs/ARCHITECTURE.md](../docs/ARCHITECTURE.md)). Humans reply with gaps of **0–300 ms** ([Stivers et al., PNAS 2009](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0903616106)). Deaf and hard-of-hearing caption users (n=216) rated captions about 1 point worse per 7.5 s of delay ([arXiv 2609.11408](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11408)); around 4 s is cited as acceptable (secondary source). So "speak now" cues are out of reach, and Aside's current chunked captions are slower than they should be. Aside suits cues that stay useful 10–30 s later, or after the talk.

Hearing the partner is the second limit. On research smart glasses, partner speech was transcribed worse than the wearer's: **20.3–24.4% word error vs 14.3–17.9%** for the wearer, and errors rose as delay shrank ([CHiME-8 MMCSG](https://www.isca-archive.org/chime_2024/zmolikova24_chime.pdf); figures parsed from a PDF table). In real far-field meetings, even the best system got about 1 word in 5 wrong once speaker errors were counted (19.90%) ([CHiME-8 DASR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.16447)). Summaries hold up much better: LLM meeting summaries built from transcripts with over 50% errors did roughly as well as ones built from ~11%-error transcripts ([MERL TR2026-008](https://www.merl.com/publications/docs/TR2026-008.pdf)). That favors "gist" features over word-exact ones. On regular Meta glasses, the Bluetooth mic mode is designed to keep the wearer's voice and suppress others. Only the Display's ambient audio may hear partners, and that path is **experimental, dev/beta only, and cannot go to production**. Distribution is capped at 100 testers ([docs/research-wearables.md](../docs/research-wearables.md); [Meta for Developers](https://developers.meta.com/blog/build-for-display-glasses/)). Partner-speech accuracy, battery, display wake-on-cue, and real end-to-end latency are all **unmeasured**.

Policy sets the third limit. The Muse API terms require **adult end users**, so Aside cannot serve teens today, even though loneliness rises in the teen years ([docs/research-model.md](../docs/research-model.md)). Meta's AUP bans emotion recognition in workplace and school settings. Eleven US states require everyone's consent to record, and whether streaming to the cloud without saving counts as "recording" is unresolved and needs a lawyer ([RecordingLaw](https://www.recordinglaw.com/party-two-party-consent-states/)). Bystander lawsuits over Meta glasses were active in Aug–Sept 2026 ([Biometric Update](https://www.biometricupdate.com/202609/metas-smart-glasses-privacy-defense-falters-when-ai-can-use-camera-without-recording-light)). In 2024, students used Ray-Ban Meta glasses to go from a stranger's face to their home address ([404 Media](https://www.404media.co/someone-put-facial-recognition-tech-onto-metas-smart-glasses-to-instantly-dox-strangers/)). Features that rely only on the wearer carry the least risk. Features that keep other people's speech carry the most.

## What to build first, what to avoid, and what to test

| Priority | Opportunity | Struggle it targets | Why it fits Aside | Key limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1 — build first** | **"What did that mean?" on request** (Analyze now → clarify cue for idioms, vague references like "Friday", possible jokes) | Implied meaning; unwritten rules | The clarify cue type already exists; a 10 s answer is fine when the user asks; it is pull-based | Text only, no tone, so sarcasm must be framed as "might be"; no accuracy data yet |
| **1** | **Pre-event briefing** ("unwritten rules" for an interview, party, or new job), shown on the phone and pinned to the glasses | Unwritten rules; interviews; anxiety | No capture, no bystander risk, no latency problem, not blocked by beta limits | LLM advice leans neurotypical; show options, not orders |
| **1** | **Wearer-written scripts, talking points, exit phrases, optional disclosure card**, shown when the user taps | Starting and leaving conversations; work | Uses existing notes/topics; the user writes the script, so it is not system-imposed masking | Notes are wiped on Stop today; needs opt-in storage |
| **2 — build next** | **Setting and sensory cues** (relative "getting louder" trends, quiet-mode reminders) plus sensory-first display defaults | Noise overload; burnout | Matches the current surroundings mode and 10–30 s timing | Audio level is uncalibrated; trends only, not true dB |
| **2** | **Captions as a processing aid**, differentiated from Meta's (e.g., "last question you were asked", a short catch-up line) | Speech in noise (top struggle) | Addresses the best-evidenced need | Needs streaming speech-to-text for 1–4 s delay; partner audio is beta-only; benefit for autistic users is unproven; Meta already ships plain captions |
| **3 — only with a consent mode** | **"What did I miss" gist and private after-talk debrief** | Slow processing; replaying talks | Summaries hold up despite transcription errors; after the talk, delay does not matter | Keeps other people's speech; all-party-consent states; the need itself has weak evidence |
| **3** | **Own-voice volume meter**, opt-in, on the phone | Volume (no data) | Wearer's own voice only | Current prompt forbids it; uncalibrated audio; risks becoming a masking tool |

Aside should avoid a long list of features, each rejected by the evidence above. That includes emotion or intent labels and face or voice identification. It also includes "speak now" turn cues (0–300 ms needed vs ~10 s available) and automatic "they're bored, time to leave" detection, which would need emotion inference. Behavior commands such as eye contact, smiling, or tone correction are out too, and so are parent, employer, or clinician dashboards. Aside should also avoid marketing to children or teens under the current API terms, and claiming benefit from unblinded self-ratings. Aside should also consider making **on-request mode the default** and treating automatic cues as a setting the wearer turns on.

Before scaling, test with autistic adults, ideally co-designed and including conversation partners. First, do glanceable cues during a conversation help or distract? No autism data exists. The Begel study hints at "cognitive overhead" ([Begel 2020](https://andrewbegel.com/papers/lessons-learned-autism.pdf)). Second, what delay can users tolerate for clarifications versus captions? Third, does use raise or lower masking effort and fatigue, measured with standard camouflaging and burnout scales? Fourth, does use survive past 1–3 months, given Superpower Glass's ~49% dropout? Fifth, does wearing visible AI glasses "out" the wearer or unsettle partners? On the hardware side, test partner-speech accuracy on Display audio, display wake-on-cue, battery drain, and real end-to-end delay.

## Conclusion

The best-supported autistic struggles split into two kinds. Some are problems of **information access**: hearing speech in noise, decoding implied meaning, learning unwritten rules. Others are problems of **how other people react**: snap judgments, rapport gaps, stigma. A wearer-only device can help with the first kind. It cannot fix the second, and trying to (by coaching the wearer to look "normal") is what autistic critics object to most. So Aside's no-emotion, no-face-ID design is not just a legal safeguard. It is the right product position: private, on-request translation and preparation that the wearer controls.

The larger point is that Aside's biggest limits and the community's wishes point the same way. The ~10 s delay, the beta-only partner audio, and the consent law all push toward preparation, clarification on request, and wearer-owned notes. Those are also the features that ask autistic users to hide the least. Nobody has yet shown that any real-time glasses aid improves autistic adults' conversations. Aside's first job is to produce that evidence with autistic users, not to assume it.
